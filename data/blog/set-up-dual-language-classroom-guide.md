Setting up a dual language classroom for the first time can feel overwhelming. You're balancing two languages, two sets of materials, and two parent communities — often with little training and even less time. This step-by-step guide walks you through the eight things to do before students arrive, in the order experienced dual language teachers recommend.

If you're still deciding whether dual language is the right model, start with our overview of [dual language classroom resources](/dual-language-classroom) to see what a fully-equipped program looks like.

## Step 1: Define Your Language Allocation Plan

Before you arrange a single desk, write down exactly how you'll allocate the two languages across the day. Common patterns:

- **50/50 by day:** Mondays/Wednesdays/Fridays in Spanish, Tuesdays/Thursdays in English (or vice versa)
- **50/50 by subject:** Math and Science in English, ELA and Social Studies in Spanish
- **90/10 K-2:** 90% Spanish in Kindergarten, 80/20 in Grade 1, 70/30 in Grade 2, ramping to 50/50 by Grade 4

Whichever you choose, **post it visibly** so substitutes, paraprofessionals, and parents can see it. Consistency matters more than perfection.

## Step 2: Plan Your Physical Layout

Divide your classroom into clearly defined areas, each labeled in both languages:

- **Reading corner / Rincón de lectura**
- **Math center / Centro de matemáticas**
- **Writing station / Estación de escritura**
- **Word wall / Pared de palabras**
- **Calendar area / Área del calendario**

Use [bilingual classroom labels](/bilingual-classroom-labels) so every visible object — desks, shelves, supply bins, doors — becomes a vocabulary lesson. Aim for label density: research suggests 50+ visible bilingual labels in a typical K-5 classroom.

## Step 3: Set Up Two Word Walls (Not One)

A common mistake is to mix English and Spanish words on a single word wall. Don't. Use **two parallel word walls** — one for each language — placed at student eye level. This makes word-finding faster and reinforces that both languages have equal status.

Update word walls weekly with 6-10 new words per language, retiring mastered words to a "Word Wall Alumni" binder students can reference.

## Step 4: Create a Bilingual Visual Schedule

Print a visual schedule strip showing the day's flow in both languages. Use pictures + text:

- 8:00 — Morning meeting / Reunión matutina
- 8:30 — Math (Spanish) / Matemáticas
- 9:30 — Reading (English) / Lectura
- 10:30 — Recess / Recreo
- ...

The visual schedule cuts down on the question "What are we doing next?" — especially valuable for newcomer students who don't yet have the English to ask it.

## Step 5: Prepare First-Week Vocabulary Cards

The first two weeks of a dual language classroom are vocabulary-intensive. Have 6-10 themed sets of bilingual vocabulary cards ready:

- Classroom objects (silla, mesa, libro, lápiz...)
- Greetings (hola, buenos días, adiós...)
- Numbers 1-20
- Colors
- Body parts
- Family members
- Feelings
- Action verbs

Each set should be printed, laminated, and stored in a labeled bin students can access during independent work. See our complete [English-Spanish printables collection](/english-spanish-printables) for ready-made bilingual vocabulary packs.

## Step 6: Prepare Bilingual Parent Communication

Send home a welcome packet in both languages **before** the first day if possible. Include:

- Welcome letter from you (in English and Spanish)
- Supply list (bilingual)
- First-week schedule
- A "About Our Family" survey (in the parent's home language)

Bilingual parent communication signals from Day 1 that both languages are valued. For ready-to-edit templates, read our [ELL parent communication templates guide](/teaching-tips/ell-parent-communication-templates-guide).

## Step 7: Plan Cross-Language Connections (Cognates)

Spanish and English share thousands of cognates — words that look and sound similar with related meanings. Build a small "Cognate Wall" near your word walls. Examples for K-2:

- family / familia
- animal / animal
- color / color
- music / música
- school / escuela

Pointing out cognates accelerates vocabulary acquisition in both directions. Even Kindergarteners can learn to spot them.

## Step 8: Establish Language Routines

Decide upfront how you'll signal language switches:

- A bilingual carpet sign you flip ("English Time" / "Hora del español")
- A bell or chime
- A specific song or chant to mark the transition
- A poster: "We are in [language] right now"

Students adapt fast — within two weeks, most K-2 learners will know which language to use based on the visual cue alone.

## A Realistic First-Month Timeline

Here's what experienced dual language teachers actually do in Month 1:

- **Week 1:** Establish routines, names, classroom tour in both languages
- **Week 2:** Introduce vocabulary cards by theme (one set per day)
- **Week 3:** Begin partner reading, simple bilingual sentence frames
- **Week 4:** Start the first content unit, weave in academic vocabulary
