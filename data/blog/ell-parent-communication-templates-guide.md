## The Communication Challenge

For many ELL families, school communication feels like a **one-way street in a language they don't understand**. Permission slips, report cards, newsletters, and conference invitations arrive in English — and parents are left guessing what they say.

This isn't just inconvenient. It creates a **fundamental barrier** between families and schools. When parents can't understand school communication, they can't:

- Support homework and learning at home
- Attend events and conferences with confidence
- Advocate for their child's needs
- Feel welcome in the school community

Bilingual communication templates solve this by meeting families where they are — in their home language.

## Types of Parent Communication

### Welcome Letters

The first communication sets the tone for the entire year. A bilingual welcome letter tells families: *"We see you. We value your language. You belong here."*

**Include in your welcome letter:**
- Teacher introduction and contact information
- Classroom schedule and routines
- How to reach the school for questions
- Invitation to participate in classroom activities

### Progress Updates

Regular progress updates keep families informed without waiting for report card season. A simple bilingual template every 2-4 weeks can cover:

- Skills the student is working on
- Areas of growth and celebration
- Specific ways parents can help at home
- Upcoming assessments or milestones

### Event Invitations

Field trips, parent-teacher conferences, school performances, and family nights all need bilingual invitations. Include:

- Date, time, and location (with a simple map if needed)
- What to bring or prepare
- RSVP instructions in both languages
- Transportation notes

### Behavior Communication

When addressing behavior issues, bilingual communication is especially critical. Misunderstandings about behavior expectations can damage the home-school relationship.

**Tip:** Always lead with something positive before addressing concerns. Frame the communication as a partnership, not a report.

## Best Practices

### 1. Keep It Simple

Use **short sentences and common words** in both languages. Avoid educational jargon that even English-speaking parents struggle with.

**Instead of:** "Your child is demonstrating emergent literacy skills in the phonemic awareness domain."

**Write:** "Your child is learning to hear and identify sounds in words. This is an important reading skill."

### 2. Use Consistent Templates

When families receive communication in a **consistent format**, they learn to navigate it even with limited language skills. Use the same layout, colors, and structure every time.

### 3. Include Visual Cues

Add icons, checkboxes, and color coding to make documents scannable. A green checkmark next to "Great job!" communicates across any language.

### 4. Provide Multiple Channels

Some families prefer paper notes. Others check text messages. Some use WhatsApp. **Offer bilingual communication through the channels families actually use.**

### 5. Invite Two-Way Communication

Don't just send information out — create ways for families to respond. Include a tear-off reply slip, a QR code to a form, or a simple "Please call/text me at _____" line.

## Building a Communication Routine

- **Weekly:** One-page bilingual class newsletter (what we learned, what's coming up)
- **Bi-weekly:** Individual student progress notes (even a sticky note in the backpack counts)
- **Monthly:** Event invitation or family engagement activity
- **Quarterly:** Formal progress report with bilingual summary
- **As needed:** Behavior communication, schedule changes, emergency information
