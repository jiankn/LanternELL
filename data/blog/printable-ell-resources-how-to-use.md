## Not All Printables Are Created Equal

The internet is full of printable worksheets, but most are designed for native English speakers. For ELL students, a typical English-only worksheet can feel like a test rather than a learning tool.

**Effective ELL printables** share these qualities:

- **Bilingual support** — key vocabulary and instructions in both languages
- **Visual scaffolding** — images, icons, and diagrams that support comprehension
- **Appropriate complexity** — matched to language proficiency level, not just grade level
- **Clear formatting** — generous white space, readable fonts, organized layout

When choosing printable resources, ask yourself: *"Could a student with limited English still understand what to DO with this page?"*

## Choosing the Right Resource

### Match Proficiency Level, Not Just Grade Level

A 4th grader who arrived last month from Guatemala and a 4th grader who's been in a bilingual program since kindergarten have very different needs — even though they're the same age.

**WIDA Level 1-2 (Entering/Emerging):** Choose printables with heavy visual support, word banks, and matching activities. Avoid long reading passages or open-ended writing prompts.

**WIDA Level 3-4 (Developing/Expanding):** Use printables that gradually increase text complexity. Fill-in-the-blank and sentence completion activities bridge the gap between visual and text-based learning.

**WIDA Level 5 (Bridging):** Students at this level can handle grade-level printables with some bilingual vocabulary support. Focus on academic language and content-specific vocabulary.

### Match the Activity Type to Your Goal

- **Vocabulary building?** → Use vocabulary cards, matching activities, word sorts
- **Speaking practice?** → Use dialogue strips, conversation prompts, sentence frames
- **Writing development?** → Use structured worksheets with sentence starters and word banks
- **Assessment?** → Use activities that show what students *know*, not just what they can say in English

## Print, Laminate, or Go Digital?

### Print and Use (Consumable)

Best for worksheets where students write, draw, or cut. Print fresh copies for each use.

**Tip:** Print double-sided when possible to save paper. Group related activities (front: vocabulary practice, back: sentence writing) for a complete lesson on one sheet.

### Laminate for Reuse

Best for vocabulary cards, labels, game pieces, and reference materials. Laminate once, use all year.

**Tip:** Use **dry-erase markers** on laminated worksheets so students can practice, wipe clean, and try again. This is especially valuable for handwriting practice and math activities.

### Digital Display

Best for whole-class instruction. Project printable resources on the board for group activities, model completion, or shared reading.

**Tip:** Zoom in on specific sections of the printable during instruction, then hand out copies for independent practice.

## Integrating Printables into Lesson Plans

Printables should never be "busy work." Here's how to make them part of meaningful instruction:

### Before the Lesson: Preview Vocabulary

Use vocabulary cards or visual supports to **pre-teach key words** before the main lesson. Spend 3-5 minutes introducing the words students will encounter.

### During the Lesson: Guided Practice

Work through the printable together first. Model how to complete it, think aloud about your choices, and let students ask questions before working independently.

### After the Lesson: Independent or Partner Practice

Students complete the printable independently or with a partner. Walk the room, providing support where needed. Avoid grading these — they're practice, not tests.

### Extension: Home Connection

Send a copy home with a brief parent note (in both languages) explaining what the student practiced and how families can reinforce it at home.

## Tracking Progress with Printables

Printable activities can double as **informal assessments** if you collect and review them regularly.

**Simple tracking system:**
- Keep a folder for each student's completed printables
- Date-stamp each one
- Every 2-3 weeks, review the folder and note patterns: What words are consistently correct? What structures are still challenging?
- Use this data to choose the next set of printables — more practice on weak areas, new challenges for mastered skills
